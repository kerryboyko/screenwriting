When an American trans woman struggling to stay in Ireland discovers that marrying her Irish goth girlfriend could solve her immigration crisis, she faces an impossible romantic dilemma: how do you propose to the woman you already want to marry without making it sound like a visa application?

## Synopsis

An American trans woman has built a life in Ireland, or at least the beginnings of one. She has a girlfriend she adores, a postgraduate qualification she never particularly wanted, and a growing collection of job rejections. Her graduate immigration permission allows her to work without immediate sponsorship, but employers keep asking whether she'll need sponsorship _eventually_. The answer is yes. The interviews never come.

Her Irish girlfriend is a goth with a dry wit and a spectacular command of profanity. The American loves her with the extravagant devotion of Gomez Addams for Morticia. She even wants to propose. Eventually. They both know it's too early, and they've agreed not to rush things.

Then the American discovers that marriage to an Irish citizen could offer a route to residence and work permission without an employer-sponsored permit. The revelation is thrilling for approximately five seconds. Now the proposal she genuinely wanted to make risks sounding like a desperate administrative maneuver.

An Irish friend attempts to explain the moral distinction: being hopelessly in love makes her a _lovely eejit_; asking someone to marry her purely to secure a visa makes her a _gobshite_, possibly a _fecking weapon_, and certainly a _daft cunt_. The American scrambles for a pen and paper. She needs this diagrammed.

Meanwhile, neither woman can afford a flat. The girlfriend lives with her parents; the American rents a room. There is, however, a front room in the family house that could theoretically become a bedroom. Suddenly a modest renovation carries the weight of a marriage settlement, and everyone — her mother, father, sister, and brother-in-law — has opinions.

As romance, housing, family expectations, and immigration bureaucracy collide, the couple must negotiate what marriage would actually mean. Their increasingly elaborate 'prenup' becomes a modern Proviso Scene: an argument about blankets, swearing, independence, devotion, and the difference between choosing a future together and being cornered into one.

The central question isn't whether they love each other. They do. It's whether they can make a free, joyful commitment while the practical stakes threaten to turn every declaration of love into a transaction.

## Comps

Congreve's restoration comedy classic _The Way of the World_ meets _The Addams Family_, with the romantic bureaucracy of _The Proposal_, the governmental absurdity of _Catch-22_ and the modern take on classic dilemmas of _Ten Things I Hate About You_.

## Themes

Love vs. Necessity. A practical benefit doesn't invalidate a genuine feeling, but desperation can distort the freedom to choose. Marriage as Contract and Romance. The institution is simultaneously emotional, legal, financial, and domestic. Home and Belonging. A place to live isn't always a place where you're allowed to stay. Immigration and Precarity. The rules governing a life can become absurdly detached from the life itself. Independence vs. Interdependence. Loving someone means choosing each other, not turning them into a solution. Language and Cultural Translation. Sometimes understanding an Irish insult requires a flowchart.

## Tagline

_For better, for worse, for richer, for poorer, and subject to immigration approval._

## Writer's Note

This is a romantic comedy about two women who are already in love. The American trans woman is the Gomez; her Irish goth girlfriend is the Morticia. Their affection isn't the question. The problem is that love exists inside systems of money, housing, immigration, and family obligation, and those systems have a habit of making sincere choices look suspiciously transactional.

The story borrows the spirit of Congreve's _The Way of the World_, particularly its Proviso Scene, without requiring a direct adaptation. Here the lovers' negotiations take the form of a prenup, and the family estate is a front room that might be converted into a bedroom.

The comedy should come from character, linguistic collisions, and the preposterous dignity with which people attempt to manage impossible practical circumstances. The American is exuberantly romantic and occasionally overanalytical; the Irishwoman is dry, gothic, affectionate, and gloriously profane. Their relationship is never the punchline.

The protagonist's transness is part of who she is, not a twist or the central obstacle. The emotional engine is the collision between wanting to ask someone to marry you and fearing that the circumstances will make the question impossible to ask honestly.

Mostly, it's about discovering that the terms and conditions of a shared life aren't the opposite of romance. Sometimes they're how two people describe the life they want to build.

(And yes, this is both autobiographical and a love letter to my girlfriend.)

Some development notes: I need to figure out the Irish woman's compelling internal dilemma — I have some ideas, all focused around the idea of "how much of herself is she willing to give up to build a life with someone she loves.

But also — and this is the real thing. This situation is happening _in real life, right now_, and the one thing I don't want to do is write _fanfic of my own life_ only to have real life not work out that way. So I want to see how real life plays out first.

There's something potentially strange about turning an unresolved personal situation into a narrative before you've finished living it. This _particular_ story can wait.

That said... I think it's still a great concept. We'll see what happens.
