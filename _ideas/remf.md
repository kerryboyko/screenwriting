When a young actuary opposed to the Vietnam War is drafted, he hopes a desk assignment in Saigon will keep him from becoming another casualty statistic. But after the Tet Offensive exposes him to the human cost of war, his attempts to prevent further bloodshed put him at odds with an Army more interested in winning battles than hearing what the numbers have to say.

## Synopsis

John Behr is a recent actuarial science graduate from Penn State, and is looking forward to starting his life. But instead, he's drafted into the Vietnam War, despite being opposed to it himself. He considers Canada, but his mother points out that if he goes, the Army will take some other mother's kid. So he shows up and is sent to Vietnam.

His attitude immediately gets him in trouble with his sergeant. Threatening him with court martial for insubordination, and told to "start acting like he's in the United States Army," Behr dares him to follow through, because "compared to Vietnam, Leavenworth might as well have spinning teacups."

The Sergeant brings him to an officer and recommends he be sent to the front-lines. When the officer asks him to "explain himself," he goes into actuarial analysis, explaining _exactly_ how he came to the conclusion that military prison was better than continuing in the army, quoting UCMJ laws and penalties, troop fatalities, likelihood of survival, etc.

The officer dismisses him, but instead of transferring him to the front lines, he's transferred to Saigon, as a "Rear Echelon M\*\*\*\*\*F\*\*\*\*\*."

He's trying to avoid becoming a statistic by becoming the man who calculates the statistics, determined to survive his two years of active duty. He places a countdown-to-freedom calendar on his desk, trying to avoid responsibility for a war he didn't choose.

But after the Tet Offensive brings the fighting to his office, he comes to realize the true human cost behind each one of the numbers on his desk.

He's more determined than ever to get back home. His C.O. says Leavenworth is still an option, but as grisly as his work has become, he's saving lives with his math... recommending the path to the quickest end to the war, through the least lives. He tells him he's exceptionally good at his job, that his work is important, and that he could make an even bigger difference if he stays for a six-month extension.

For the first time since being drafted, Behr voluntarily chooses to stay in Vietnam, actively participating because he's found a moral justification for continuing to do something he originally believed was wrong.

That is, until he's handed the analysis for a military operation on an objective known as "Hill 937." He calculates that the cost in lives won't justify the operation, and sends it through the system. The Army goes ahead with the plan anyway, and the casualties are so great that the soldiers start to call it "Hamburger Hill." When the army finally takes the hill, at great cost, they almost immediately abandon it, leaving the NVA to move back in.

John learns the Army disregarded his analysis, and abandoned the position, through an updated operational assessment.

He asks why.

All he gets back is: "The strategic situation has changed."

Utterly disillusioned, the only way he can survive is by forcing himself to once again dehumanize the numbers he works with, because thinking of them as _people_ is psychologically unbearable, knowing that there is nothing he can do to prevent casualties, end the war faster, or make the slightest bit of difference.

Finally, his time is up. His C.O. asks him to re-up. He returns home.

He looks his mother dead in the eye: "I should have gone to Canada."

### Comps

_Paths of Glory_ (1957) meets _The Report_ (2019), with the moral calculus of _Eye in the Sky_ (2015). The second half of _Full Metal Jacket_ for the atmosphere ov Vietnam and the disintegration of the protagonist's worldview.

### Themes

Anti-War. The way that systems dehumanize people — even the people participating in the system. The cost of compliance vs. the cost of defiance. Human stupidity combined with military arrogance.

### Tagline

_Every number is somebody's son._

### Writer's Note

My dad did two tours as an enlistee in the Vietnam War. He didn't like to talk about it, but one time I overheard him talking with a fellow Vietnam vet about how even on the back lines, they were doing things like pouring their drinks through cheesecloth because Viet Cong sympathizers were putting shattered glass into it. I have no idea if the story is true, but I'm always fascinated by stories that take the familiar narrative we _think_ we know and force us to look at it from a different perspective. After all, that's what _Wicked_ did to _The Wizard of Oz_, what _Hamilton_ did for _1776_, what _Star Wars: The Force Awakens_ tried to do (without much success) by making the deuteragonist a stormtrooper.

This is a story about a person caught seeing the flaws in a system he's utterly powerless to change and utterly vulnerable to, trying at first to use his mind to survive it, only to find that intelligence is not a survival skill against institutional priorities.

John Behr doesn't actually become a better soldier. He becomes a better human being, then the Army teaches him that being a better human being is irrelevant to its objectives.
