_Greybeard_ follows a team of veteran wizards working for a magical consultancy, brought in to fix other people’s arcane disasters in a modern fantasy world where nobody fully understands the systems they depend on and everyone wants someone else to take responsibility.

## Synopsis

Joanna Brandycorn is the latest wunderkind hire straight out of wizard grad school. She is hired by Arcane Solutions, a magical consultancy, and is assigned to the nearly burnt-out, sardonic, and irascible Johannes Greybeard. Greybeard is less interested in the new and exciting ideas Joanna proposes than he is in making sure that nobody breaks a mission-critical spell or accidentally summons anything that can file a grievance.

This is a process comedy about wizards who act like computer programmers, _Greybeard_ thrives on the moments when the system resets, the job is never done, competence is provisional, and every episode answers the question: "What broke this time?" 

The wizards of the fantasy realm are highly sought out, highly compensated professionals, but magic itself is brittle, barely documented infrastructure maintained by exhausted specialists who do not trust it. Spells never work right on the first try. There's always a new bug. Standards and best practices have been established to avoid catastrophe. Failure is expected. Confidence is suspicious. And everyone is polite because if they aren't, reality might segfault. 

In other words, they're wizards who have the job of software engineers, only the undocumented legacy systems they're working with are the laws of physics and metaphysics. 

Sometimes nobody knows if the spell does what it claims. Sometimes there's another damn rune you have to learn to keep up with the latest magic trends. And the most dangerous wizard isn't the one who calls himself Archmage — it's the one that has admin access. 

This is a world with spells that _technically_ succeed, but in ways nobody anticipated because the spec was wrong, not the execution. 

Underneath the comedy, the show is about people maintaining systems they didn't design, inheriting bad decisions from legendary predecessors, being blamed when invisible work fails, and being terrified of making changes because _what if this breaks everything?_. That's not just programmers (or wizards). That's adulthood. 

#### Potential characters: 

* Johannes Greybeard — Senior Consultant. He's a legacy architect and basically Gandalf who would _really_ like to lie down. He *can* fix things. He *won't* unless absolutely necessary. He's not cruel or lazy, he just understands triage, and knows that the minute he touches a spell, he owns it. 

* Joanna Brandycorn — New Hire / Junior Consultant. She's being hopeful without being stupid, which is the hardest job in the room. She believes problems _should_ be solvable but has just enough skill to make things worse. She's our POV character, asking the questions everyone else stopped asking because the answers were too depressing. She's not naive. She's _pre-disillusioned_. Most importantly, she's better at noticing human consequences and remembering why people asked for help in the first place. 

* Abigail McGillicuddy-Luna — Reliability Witch / lead of Warl-ops. Never surprised. She never says "This is bad," she says "I told you this would happen." She knows what breaks, remembers outages everyone else forgot, does preventive rituals nobody appreciates and still gets blamed anyway. 

* Brother Jacob of the Secret Covenant — Compliance Cleric. He's kind. Sincere. Friendly. And absolutely immovable. He doesn't enforce rules because he enjoys power, he enforces them because he believes rules are what keep the gods from noticing. He genuinely likes the team. That's what makes him dangerous. 

* Chlodmer Hogpen — Rogue Pen-Tester. Chlodmer is the odd duck in the office, the one who is _happy_ when he figures out how something breaks... by breaking it. He's religiously literal in a world built on euphemism and denial. Rules, to him, are hypotheses to be tested. He delights in unintended consequences. He's not reckless. He's _curious without fear._ The reason he's invaluable? He documents _everything_ he's learned. 

### Comps

_Discworld_ meets _Silicon Valley_ with the pacing of _The Thick Of It_, and the episodic structure of _Brooklyn Nine-Nine_. 

### Themes

Perceived vs. actual authority. Complex systems survive because people who don’t get credit are afraid of what happens if they stop. 

### Taglines

_I don’t know what the spell does. I know what happens if we stop casting it._

_They're the team that keeps the lights on. Though, in their case, the lights are stars._

### Writer's Note

The idea of wizards as programmers is familiar. But there's a difference between borrowing the vocabulary of software engineering for magical jokes and actually understanding how engineering organizations function. I've been a software engineer for ten years, I think I might have a few ideas how to tackle the problem.
