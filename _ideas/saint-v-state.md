When a vampire is executed without trial—exposing the existence of vampires to the world—a retired civil rights attorney is asked by his estranged, undead ex-lover to bring a landmark case. What begins as a plea for due process becomes a test of who counts as human—and whether justice can survive fear.

## Synopsis

The story begins where most vampire tales end: with the villainous Ambrose immolated by the “good guys” in a windowed cell. But when Sebastian Saint, Ambrose’s principled childe, approaches his estranged former lover—Mark, a semi-retired civil rights lawyer—he pleads for a posthumous suit arguing even monsters deserve due process. 

Mark, now married to his husband Marcel, resists. He’s afraid of vampires, and of Sebastian. Furthermore, he worries about his reputation if he takes on a vampire as a client. But Marcel, a survivor of the AIDS years, reminds him: when they were teenagers, Mark and Sebastian never had the language for their love. To deny Sebastian now would repeat that silence. And silence = death.

As hysteria spreads and innocents—human and undead—begin vanishing, Mark realizes this isn’t about Ambrose. It’s about whether rights mean anything if the state can decide who is, and isn’t, human. He takes the case.

In court, Sebastian faces Irene Fletcher, a prosecutor who views him as both legally dangerous and personally repulsive. Yet as the hearing unfolds, Irene comes to respect Sebastian’s dignity, while Sebastian admires her hunger for justice—even as they clash over what justice means. 

Cameras can’t pick him up, so they use LIDAR. He can’t take an oath on the Bible, so he affirms. Sketch artists, thermal imaging, affidavits from physicians with EKGs showing zero over zero — the system is stretched until it becomes absurd. And the absurdity only underlines the stakes. 

Sebastian is quick-witted, sardonic, and unflinching. He jokes because he has to — not to amuse, but to survive. The question he keeps asking: “Do I deserve justice, even if I scare you?” 

The courtroom becomes both crucible and stage, as Sebastian risks everything to prove that rights either apply to everyone... or they’re not rights at all. 

Mark and Sebastian win the case. But as they leave the courthouse, a mob storms the building, drags Sebastian outside, handcuffs him to a tree, and shoots him with wooden arrows—until one pierces his heart. 

The court recognizes Sebastian's rights.

The mob refuses to.

And the law that has just affirmed his personhood cannot protect him from people determined to deny it.

(Imagery and story borrow heavily from the tale of Christian martyr St. Sebastian.)

## Short Film Variant

The short film variant would be a single scene: Sebastian heads to give a videotaped deposition, only for the defense to find out that Sebastian doesn't show up on videotape, and they have to engineer a system out of LIDAR in order to do the deposition. The title for that would likely be: _Deposition With The Vampire_.

### Comps

_Philadelphia_ meets _Let the Right One In_. _Miracle on 34th Street_ (but bitter), _The People vs. Larry Flynt_

### Themes

How secure are human rights when the state can arbitrarily decide who is, and who isn't, human? Inequality under the law. The importance of due process, even for the guilty. Mob mentality and fear. What happens when the person whose civil rights you're defending really is a monster?

_Sunlight Is Not Justice_

### Writer's Note:

This was my "backup" idea to write a feature for the M.A. Screenwriting programme at the National Film School at IADT, but eventually chose [_Polite Company_]({{ '/projects/polite-company/' | relative_url }}){: .marked_link }. 

I remember lecturer and programme chair Paul Freaney looking over my proposed synopsis and balking at the comps where I called it "_Philadelphia_ meets _Let The Right One In_" and said that "that made no sense! Those movies are nothing like each other!" 

I told him the apparent incompatibility between _Philadelphia_ and _Let the Right One In_ is precisely what makes _Saint v. State_ an interesting premise. I _almost_ chose it over [_Polite Company_]({{ '/projects/polite-company/' | relative_url }}){: .marked_link } simply to show it could be done. 

One other thing: I decided that Sebastian should possess a quick dry wit almost immediately. I believe comedy isn't the opposite of seriousness—it's the opposite of sentimentality. He's not using humor to escape the moment, but to survive it. 

I can imagine a scene where Sebastian is chided by Fletcher for "not taking this seriously," only for Sebastian to reply that he is taking it seriously. He's aware of how the judge, the prosecution, even his own council look at him sometimes: as a monster. The jokes are what he needs to be seen as a human. To be less of a threat. "My frivolity is anything but frivolous, and your sincerity, Ms. Fletcher, is a priviledge I myself do not possess." 

Ultimately, this is a story about human rights. It’s about who gets rights, and who gets due process, when the public is scared. That’s about trans people. It’s about asylum seekers. It’s about people with criminal records, mental illness, or "foreign-sounding" names. Sebastian’s trial is about vampires — but it’s also about everyone who’s ever been told: "You’re not one of us, so the law doesn’t apply to you."

I'm also aware that this is my third project involving vampires. It's true. I love them, but not because I love horror, or because I want to indulge in fantasies of sex or power. No—vampires aren't just a metaphor — they’re a pressure test. They carry associations with queerness, contagion, outsider status, fear of the unknown. 

It’s not _about_ transphobia, or homophobia, or xenophobia. It’s about what _all_ the _phobias_ — the Greek word for ‘fear’ — have in common: the social fear of the ‘other,’ and the trauma of _being othered._ That includes racism, ableism, misogyny… _ad infinitum._
