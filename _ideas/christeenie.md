When a hyper-rational software engineer offers to become a donor for her dying mother, she discovers she's a fae changeling, switched at birth. The only compatible donor is the human woman raised in her place, somewhere in the fae realms. To save her mother, she must navigate a labyrinth of riddles, contracts, and ancient rules that make no logical sense—until she starts treating magic like badly written code.

## Synopsis

Christine "Chris" is a practical, methodical software engineer who spends her working life solving problems with logic, then unwinds by losing herself in books, films, and television. When her mother becomes desperately ill, Chris offers to donate what she needs to survive. The tests reveal something neither of them could have anticipated: Chris isn't biologically related to her mother. In fact, she isn't human.

Chris was switched at birth with another Christine: "Teenie," a human raised among the fae. And Teenie may be the only genetic match who can save the woman Chris knows as her mother.

Finding Teenie means entering a fae realm governed by ancient bargains, literal-minded contracts, and an elaborate hierarchy of riddles. Chris discovers a world where everyone is bound by rules, but nobody seems interested in whether those rules make sense. Her instinct isn't to master the magic. It's to debug it.

Among the guardians she encounters are Jack Knight, who must always tell the truth, and his brother Jack Knave, who must always lie. Their seemingly simple riddle becomes considerably less simple when Chris introduces them to the difference between zero, `null`, `undefined`, and `NaN`. Jack Knave, who insists he isn't developing feelings for her, may be particularly affected by the encounter.

Teenie, meanwhile, is no stranger to the system's absurdities. She spends her days navigating fae riddles and trickery, and her evenings writing essays on reforming the realm's institutions that she never publishes. Where Chris uses logic professionally and escapes into fantasy, Teenie lives inside fantasy and dreams of a more rational world.

Together, they confront a system that insists each belongs in the world of her birth rather than the one that raised her. But neither wants to be assigned an identity by blood, tradition, or a bureaucratic technicality. Why shouldn't they be able to choose where they live, and who they want to be?

Chris's mother survives. Chris and Teenie gain the freedom to belong to both worlds. And Christmas dinners become considerably more awkward.

### Comps

Jim Henson's _Labyrinth_, if the labyrinth were designed by Joseph Heller. _Alice in Wonderland_ with a software engineer trying to debug Wonderland.

### Themes

Identity versus origin. Nature, nurture, and self-determination. The absurdity of inherited institutions. Rules versus justice. The difference between understanding a system and accepting its legitimacy. The right to choose where—and with whom—we belong.

### Tagline

_Two women, switched at birth. Refusing to accept that their destinies are a fae accompli._

### Writer's Note

The premise began with a changeling story: a woman discovers that the mother who raised her isn't her biological mother, and must find the human child who was taken in her place. What interested me was less the revelation of her origins than what happens afterward. Why should either woman be obliged to return to the world she was supposedly meant for?

I originally imagined Chris as a lawyer, but I've been considering making her a software engineer instead. It's a world I know better, and there's something appealing about a programmer confronting an ancient magical bureaucracy and recognizing all the familiar problems of a badly designed system: contradictory requirements, undocumented behavior, and rules that nobody dares change because everything else depends on them.

There's a particular scene I want to write in which Chris explains `NaN` to two riddle guardians using a toilet-roll holder and a fish. It's ridiculous, but it's also the argument at the heart of the story: a system's categories aren't necessarily the limits of reality.

Ultimately, Chris and Teenie don't need to trade places. They need the freedom to decide for themselves where they belong. And their mother needs to live, because the ending I want isn't a tragic lesson about accepting fate. It's a happy ending in which everyone has to negotiate the seating arrangements at Christmas.
