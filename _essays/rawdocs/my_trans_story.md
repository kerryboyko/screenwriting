This is an interesting story, because this book is the one that, in some respects, started my journey as a screenwriter and author.

I've often said that while I've written non-fiction for most of my life, I didn't start writing _fiction_ until about a year after I came out to myself as trans. The reasoning was simple: I found it hard to write relatable characters when I couldn't even relate to myself.

But that's the short version. The longer version: Not only could I not relate to myself, it was incredibly difficult to relate to _any_ fictional character. It was a matter of representation: for the most part, trans characters aren't shown on the screen. Not in movies, not in television. And while _lately_ there have been some... _well meaning attempts_... at bringing trans people onto the screen, it is still not even remotely adequate.

What we have right now is not _trans stories_ but _the_ "trans story." The one that's familiar. The one that conforms to preconceptions of what trans people look like.

The trans person is almost never the protagonist, usually a side character whom the cisgender protagonist must "learn to tolerate.". When they are promoted to deuteragonist, it's always the same story.

- They knew at a young age, and displayed signs such as crossdressing as a child.
- They got onto hormone replacement therapy young.
- And they are absolutely beautiful and stunning. The trans woman is a princess, the trans man is a handsome rogue.

There's no room in that story for the person who didn't figure it out in middle age. No room in that story for someone who still likes presenting as their gender at birth, while _feeling_ very much not.

And when the trans stories are presented, they're presented either _during_ or _after_ their transition journey. We never see those years of doubt and pain. We never see the questioning, the self-recriminations. We never see the depression and dysphoria on the screen.

This is especially true even of well-meaning allies. The character of Rose Noble on "Doctor Who" was in many ways a perfect example, and she was created by Russell T. Davies, the mind behind "Queer As Folk." There was no hatred there, but the ignorance was on full display.

Rose's biggest pain point with being trans is with the _transphobia_ in society. Not with having a body which doesn't match or feel the way it should. Not with the internal struggle of having a brain trying to run on the wrong hormones. (Testosterone literally _poisons_ my brain chemistry, estrogen has been the most effective anti-depressant I've ever taken.) And Rose being trans was merely incidental to the story or the character. She very easily could have been replaced by a cis woman (or even a cis man!) and nothing about her would really have changed.

In short, Russell T. Davies wrote Rose Noble as if she was a cis gay man, rather than as a trans woman. Which makes perfect sense, as Davies _is_ a gay man, and I can't blame him for writing what he knows.

But that's also why I think trans people need to tell our own stories. Why we need to be fighting to get our films made and our books published and our television shows out on the airwaves.

When I was a child, there were little to no trans stories on television or in the movies. When they did happen, they portrayed trans people as jokes, monsters, sexual deviants, victims, villains... anything but a fully-fleshed character in their own right. They were always "the other."

I mean, Dr. Frank N. Furter is _fun_, but he's still the _baddie._

My own egg didn't crack until I saw a trans woman's story that I _could_ relate to: Mae Dean's. [In her webcomic, "Real Life Comics"](https://www.reallifecomics.com/comic-mobile.php?comic=june-29-2020){: .marked_link }, she explained exactly what caused her to realize that she was a trans woman, and the way she explained it, it was like she had a direct line to my brain.
