Sometimes a writer would really like to play in someone else's playground.

Actually, given the popularity of [AO3](https://archiveofourown.org){: .marked_link }, that "sometimes" should be upgraded.

These aren't formal pitches, and I don't own the rights to any of these properties. They're simply stories I've loved, thought about, and occasionally tried my hand at adapting.

If the opportunity ever came along to work with the rights holders and bring one of them to the screen, I'd jump at it. Until then, consider this a peek inside my creative brain.

**Enter at your own risk.**

## [The Cuckoo's Egg: Tracking a Spy Through The Maze Of Computer Espionage, by Clifford Stoll](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cuckoo%27s_Egg_(book)>){: .marked_link }

### Format: Feature Film or Prestige TV Miniseries

<img
src="{{ '/assets/images/books/the_cuckoos_egg.jpg' | relative_url }}"
alt="The Cuckoos Egg"
class="book-float-left"
/>

If you're not familiar with it, this is the story of one of the earliest and best-documented cases of international computer espionage, and it has all the elements of a cold war spy thriller, a modern techno-thriller, and an eccentric character at its core, [astronomer Clifford Stoll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_Stoll){: .marked_link }.

In 1986, at the height of Reagan's Cold War, Stoll was managing computers at Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory in California when the accounting department flagged a discrepancy of just $0.75. Stoll pulled at that thread to realize that the error came from an unauthorized user who used nine seconds of computer time without paying for it.

As he started investigating, he figured out that someone unauthorized had acquired 'superuser' access to connect to other computers — many of them military or governmental — related to U.S. nuclear capabilities or the Strategic Defense Initiative.

This story has been the subject of multiple documentaries, including a docudrama episode of WGBH's _NOVA_ entitled ["The KGB, The Computer, and Me," (Full Video on YouTube)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vW8YBrH4Q6o){: .marked_link } featuring Stoll himself, but a pure dramatization has yet to hit the screen.

### Here's why I think I would be a good person to adapt this:

I'm a software engineer by trade, and have worked in the tech industry for twenty years, including ten in technical marketing communication. I know how to make boring details _riveting_. (Spending ten years making hard drive caching and network monitoring systems seem _interesting_ is excellent practice!)

The difference between explaining nine seconds of unauthorized computer usage and making an audience care desperately about nine seconds of unauthorized computer usage is the difference between technical exposition and dramatic storytelling. A television audience doesn't need to understand every technical detail of an intrusion. It needs to understand why the detail matters. _That_, I believe I'm well positioned to deliver.

### How I would adapt it:

I would attempt to tell two stories at once, first, the actual techno-thriller aspects of the investigation that exposed Markus Hess. But more than that, it's also a story about bureaucratic failure and a lack of imagination — as agencies at first dismissed the quirky astronomer and his $0.75 problem, then had no idea who had jurisdiction, then had no idea what to do when jurisdiction was established. I'd take the material as darkly comic on its face — a real-world Doctor Strangelove that actually happened in 1986.

As a dramatized feature film or miniseries, there's an amazing engine built right into this story: The stakes keep escalating, from an accounting problem to international espionage, while the original $0.75 remains _hilariously trivial._

Prestige historical thriller, with the institutional absurdity of Kubrick.

## [UHF](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UHF_(film)>){: .marked_link }

### Format: 60-minute TV comedy-drama

<img
src="{{ '/assets/images/movieposters/uhf.jpg' | relative_url }}"
alt="UHF"
class="book-float-left"
/>

The original movie was critically crucified when it came out and has since become a cult classic. And it's not hard to see why.

From a screenwriting standpoint, the plot is a threadbare, even _hackneyed_ 80s story of the independent business owner versus the large corporate bully. In many ways, it is a _parody_ of the format... but one that reviewers at the time examined it through a non-ironic lens.

It was also really just a way to slap a bunch of unrelated television parody sketches into a movie, even by the screenwriter, Jay Levey's own admission.

### I actually contacted Mr. Levey about doing an adaptation.

I prepared a one-page pitch for the property and approached the director Jay Levey, asking for his blessing to pursue an adaptation.

<div class="letter-dark" markdown="1">

I'm Kerry Ann Burke, an American comedy writer and former stand-up comedian...

What I've always loved about UHF, beyond the absurdity, is that Channel 62 is an institution built by outsiders whose supposed defects become their greatest strengths. George doesn't triumph by learning to fit into R.J. Fletcher's world; he builds a place where Stanley, Pamela, Raul, Philo, Kuni, Noodles, and the rest can become television stars.

I think I've found a concept for a television series that preserves that spirit: a lunatic collective of media creatives producing its own programming and building a community along the way. Rather than simply recreating the film, it would use the comic engine of UHF to take on today's media landscape with the same irreverence the original brought to television and movies of the 1980s.

It's also material I feel particularly connected to. I'm a neurodivergent trans woman who came out in middle age, and I've spent much of my life as a creative misfit. My comedy tends toward genre parody, media satire, absurd premises played absolutely seriously, and stupid jokes constructed with alarming precision—which is probably why UHF has remained so dear to me.

I'd like to write a pilot for a UHF television series on spec, but before developing the idea further, I wanted to approach you first—to get your personal and creative blessing. (IP rights are a separate issue, and I don’t presume your blessing would grant them.)

I've prepared a one-page pitch outlining the concept. If you're open to considering it, may I send it to you?

</div>

Now, sadly, he pointed out that he didn't have the rights, and he wasn't interested in revisiting the project. It was one of the nicer rejection letters I've gotten:

<div class="letter-dark" markdown="1">

Hi Kerry Ann -

It’s nice to hear from you, thank you for reaching out. I’m glad to know our little movie gave you inspiration; it’s always gratifying when we hear stories like yours.

We really have no blessings to give since, as you’ve acknowledged, we have no ownership in or control over the IP. The only reason we would weigh in is if we were directly involved in the project, which we would not really have interest in.

Wishing you the very best,

Jay Levey <br/>President, Imagine Entertainment.

</div>

Such a nice letter.

But I didn't simply file the pitch away after being rejected. One of the things I discovered was that the part of the adaptation I was most excited about wasn't actually UHF itself. It was the format, characters, and central conceit I'd invented for my proposed series.

In other words, the adaptation attempt became a development process for an original series.

I removed all traces of UHF, Weird Al, and the rest and took the original elements I had created, and with them, developed a new show pitch from the ashes: [Over The Top]({{ 'screenwriting/ideas/over-the-top/' | relative_url }}){: .marked_link }

It's funny, really. I set out to adapt a film about a bunch of outsiders who build their own television station because the established media world has no place for them.

And when the established rights situation left me without a path to that adaptation, I effectively said:

"Fine. I'll build my own television station."

</div>

## [Welcome To Dorley Hall, by Alyson Greaves](https://www.waterstones.com/book/welcome-to-dorley-hall/alyson-greaves/9781806770939){: .marked_link }

### Format: 60 minute TV Drama

<img
src="{{ '/assets/images/books/welcome_to_dorley_hall.jpg' | relative_url }}"
alt="Welcome To Dorley Hall"
class="book-float-left"
/>

This is an interesting story, because this book is the one that, in some respects, started my journey as a screenwriter and author.

I've often said that while I've written non-fiction for most of my life, I didn't start writing _fiction_ until about a year after I came out to myself as trans. The reasoning was simple: I found it hard to write relatable characters when I couldn't even relate to myself.

This was compounded by the fact that there weren't many trans characters in media to begin with, and 99% of them were jokes, monsters, sexual deviants, victims, villains... anything but the hero. And the few times when they were more than just cardboard, it was always set _during_ or _after_ the transition journey. We never see the _before._ We never see those years of doubt and pain. We never see the questioning, the self-recriminations. We never see the depression and dysphoria on the screen. And that's what I lived with for 43 years.

My own egg didn't crack until I saw a trans woman's story that I _could_ relate to: Mae Dean's. [In her webcomic, "Real Life Comics"](https://www.reallifecomics.com/comic-mobile.php?comic=june-29-2020){: .marked_link }, she explained exactly what caused her to realize that she was a trans woman, and the way she explained it, it was like she had a direct line to my brain.

Through her, I found trans activist <a href="https://www.instagram.com/transsalamander/" class="marked_link">Kat Gibes <span class="social-icon" style="--icon-url: url('{{ '/assets/images/icon_svgs/instagram.svg' | relative_url }}')" aria-hidden="true"></span></a>, and through her, I found out about _The Sisters of Dorley_ series.

In it, a trans woman infiltrates a "forced feminization" facility where toxic and violent men are kidnapped and "rehabilitated" as women, in order to have a shot at transitioning.

Before I go further: You have to understand that _Dorley_ is very much marmite. It deals with deeply uncomfortable topics. The entire thing was designed, at first, to be a short story satire that a torture dungeon would literally be more human than how England and the NHS deals with trans people. So part of that is going into detail about how a torture dungeon and kidnapping ring _would actually work,_ what would _motivate_ people to do it.

And Greaves started out examining the ethics of it, but moved away from it to focus on the interior lives of the people trapped — some literally, some metaphorically — in the system she had created.

It's roughly a million words long at this point, and while that provides _plenty_ of material for an adaptation, each chapter tends to start two plot lines for every one it advances.

That said, early on, it captured my imagination, mostly because of early chapters when stories about the early days of the events before the novel were _told_, not _shown_, and I thought it would be a fun hobby to try to _adapt_ that kind of story to television.

I basically did a "fan-adaptation" of the story, and entitled it _Dorley Girls._

And because no one told me I wasn't supposed to do this, I wrote 43 episodes a 2 hour Christmas Special, and a crossover with _the Muppets_.

That's not merely dabbling in screenwriting. That's building an entire television production slate before somebody got around to telling me how television works.

Amateurish? Yes. Because I was an amateur then. Every pro starts as an amateur... but I loved the pacing and format of screenwriting. And I think I still do. Now that I know more about structure, pacing... I can see many of the flaws with the original adaptation I made.

But I also see some really good choices that I think were actually quite good.

### Here's why I think I would be a good person to adapt this:

Because I'm trans, first of all, and this book helped start my journey into fiction writing. More than that though, I think I understand what Greaves has done that only _she_ could do: tell a compelling story about what it's like to be trans _before_ you even get the opportunity to transition — and how easily that opportunity can be robbed from you by hatred disguised as bureaucracy, or by simple things such as circumstance.

### How I would adapt it:

I would focus primarily on the conspiracy/thriller aspects. The tension comes from how this group manages to keep itself secret, especially since every year, the conspiracy gets larger and more fragile.

I would also create more variety and definition among the starting characters, and do my best to have them develop _each other's_ journeys instead of stopping to focus on one character for long stretches at a time, which is key to ensemble writing.

More than that, I would put ticking-clock pressure by having people (I used Russ and Shahida for this) investigate the facility from the outside, closing in on the conspiracy.

More importantly, I would compress the _hell_ out of the story.

The biggest departure I'd make from the source material, of course, is that by the third or fourth book in the series, Greaves more or less stops examining the _ethics_ of the facility, and more or less just goes on the journey to examine the characters. I think that would not work as a television series, as the tension and conflict of discovery drives the engine.

Most of all, I think that Greaves has told a story with a particular audience in mind: trans people who view the basement of Dorley Hall as _wish fulfillment_, rather than a _horror story._

Because it's both. There's overgeneralization here, but _Dorley_ is a _horror story_ and _wish fulfillment_ for trans people. And the interesting part is that different people will interpret the same coercive institution differently depending on their experiences, desires, and fears.
