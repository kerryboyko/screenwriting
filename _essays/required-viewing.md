When I was accepted to the National Film School at IADT, I was really worried that I'd be behind the other students. 

The truth is, I wasn't a "film buff." I didn't have knowledge of cinema history, I hadn't really watched a whole bunch of old movies or pretentious boring French films. I didn't even know where to start. 

This was mostly because I didn't like feature films. I liked to tell long, involved stories, with characters that changed over time and ensemble casts. I've been a fan of serialized TV since _Star Trek: Deep Space 9_ and _Babylon 5_. I knew that's where I wanted to work. 

I've since learned to value and appreciate films on their own merits. I guess that's one of the things they teach you in film school. 

In fact, I didn't realize how much I had changed until I visited the incoming class of IADT's film school, and sat down for lunch with them. They called me a "cinema encyclopaedia" and I was shocked, because I had indeed become one. 

Looking back at it, however, what I wish someone had done for me was compile a list of required viewing before heading into grad school. Not just classics, either, but movies that, if you see them before your lectures, you'll understand more about what the lecture is trying to teach you. 

And if everyone in the classroom has seen the same films, you'll be better able to communicate ideas to your peers by referencing the vocabulary of cinema. You may not know quite yet what a "oner" is yet, but "like that scene in _Goodfellas_ where they never cut away" explains the same idea. Or, for that matter, you may not have yet encountered "dramatic irony" as a concept, but "like how Truman in _The Truman Show_ doesn't know he's on TV, but the audience does" is doing a lot of heavy work. 

I'll be adding more to this list as I have time.

_Back To The Future_ is not the best movie ever made. It's not my favorite. But from a screenwriting craft perspective, it's hard to find a better example. 

Every line of dialogue, every bit of action is _doing something._ It's a movie about time and it doesn't waste any of it. Things often do two or three things at once. For example, early on, Mr. Strickland doesn't just admonish Marty McFly for being late to school. He says:

Look at what we're learning from that one exchange: 

* Authority figures see Marty McFly as someone who will never succeed.
* Marty wants to prove them wrong. 
* His father is someone who didn't succeed. 
* Marty McFly plays in a band (setting up his use of his guitar playing skills to get his parents to fall in love later in the film.)
* "History is gonna change" is the entire premise of the movie -- and it sets up both literal and dramatic irony. We know this is a time travel movie. Marty doesn't. So when he says that, he doesn't realize how on-the-nose he's being. 

Pick any scene in _Back To The Future_ and it will often be doing multiple things at once. When Marty wakes his father from a dead sleep using Van Halen, it not only advances the plot, it also establishes Marty's character as an outside-the-box thinker _and_ provides a comedic beat _and_ provides character motivation for George _and_ sets up the joke at the end of the movie where it serves as inspiration for George's sci-fi novel. 

And it's got dead-on three-act structure and pacing. Act I sets up Marty as down on his luck and establishes the circumstances. We break into Act II when he travels accidentally to 1955. In the middle, (literally... this scene ends at the 56:00 mark of a 1:12:00 film... not including the credits), we get the big mid-point turn:

* Marty shows Doc the video he took in 1985. 
* Doc learns the DeLorean requires 1.21 gigawatts, and establishes that finding a power source for that in 1955 is impossible, except for a bolt of lightning... "except you don't know where and when one will strike." 
* Then Marty mentions that he has a life in 1985, and a girlfriend. "Look what she wrote," he says, showing Doc the very same piece of paper that has the "Save The Clocktower" fundraiser on it, which lists *exactly* when a lightning bolt will strike. 
  * Notably, the audience gets to see the "Save The Clocktower" text clearly in frame. 
* Doc then warns Marty not to interact with anything in 1955, lest he change the future. 
* That's when Marty mentions running into his parents, and Doc establishes through the "erasing" picture that Marty has accidentally erased himself from the timeline. 

The film has already established: 

* that the DeLorean needs an extraordinary amount of electricity to travel through time, 
* that lightning struck Hill Valley's clock tower at a particular time in 1955. 

Until this scene, however, those two facts haven't been connected. And the connection comes from something seemingly unrelated to either one: Marty's girlfriend.

Marty shows Doc the message Jennifer wrote on the clock tower flyer, reminding us of what he stands to lose if he can't return to 1985. But the very thing that establishes the emotional stakes also provides the solution to the practical problem. The flyer tells Doc exactly when and where lightning will strike.

And just as they've discovered a way home, the screenplay introduces another complication: Marty has inadvertently endangered his own existence.

There is no fat in the movie. Everything does multiple things at once. 

It took a _lot_ of hard work to write a script _that tightly packed_ with _everything_ you need, when you need it. 

I think if you watch _one_ film, just _one,_ this is the film that most clearly shows off the _craft skills_ you are going to film school to learn.

{% capture my_cousin_vinny %}
Another film to recommend because of excellence in craft is _My Cousin Vinny_. Like _Back To The Future_, every line and action in this movie accomplishes something. 

But _My Cousin Vinny_ does something magical. Without it becoming repetitive, without it becoming boring, _My Cousin Vinny_ constantly reminds you of the _stakes_ of the film. Like the arrow in the FedEx logo, you don't notice it until someone points it out. But every other scene tends to remind us what's at stake: The defendants are facing _the death penalty._ 

Additionally, it does something wonderful with those stakes: It continues to pile on _more_ as the story progresses. 

At first, it's just the fate of the boys. 

But soon, Vinny compounds the problem and has problems added for him. 

This is one of the most famous scenes in the film, and it's funny _precisely_ because Vinny is doing little more than listing all the things that can go wrong. Every item on that list is another reminder of just how much is riding on the outcome of this case.

{% include fountain.html content=biological_clock %}

_My Cousin Vinny_ uses its escalating stakes as a source of comedy, rather than allowing the comedy to _distract_ from those stakes. The audience laughs at the accumulation of problems, but the underlying danger never disappears. That's hard to pull off.

## Learning From Imperfection

<!-- Star Wars Episode I: The Phantom Menace -->

{% capture the_phantom_menace %}

I firmly believe this is a must-watch for any film student. And then after they've watched it, they should immediately consult the [Red Letter Media review of the movie](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FxKtZmQgxrI). 

That review is a film school education in and of itself. 

One particular excercise they did was ask people to describe the characters of _Phantom Menace_ and the original _Star Wars_ without describing their outfit or job in the movie. 

Han Solo: cocky thief with a heart of gold. C3PO: Prissy worrier, bumbling comic relief. 

Then they tried to do it with Qui-Gon Jinn or Princess Amidala. Crickets. 

In truth, we in filmmaking are _spoiled_ by our choice of medium. It is _expensive_ to make a film. So it is extremely rare to see a film _this bad_ and _this accessible_. Hollywood's financial gatekeeping produces a kind of survivorship bias. Most films that get substantial budgets have already passed through multiple layers of creative scrutiny. Even mediocre films tend to exhibit some baseline professional competence.

_The Phantom Menace_ is a particularly interesting case study. Unlike _Plan 9 from Outer Space_, its director wasn't constrained by budget. Unlike _The Room_, it wasn't made by an inexperienced amateur. George Lucas had already revolutionized cinema, had virtually unlimited resources, some of the finest technical artists in the world, and extraordinary creative control. 

And yet, it gets _almost none_ of the screenwriting basics right. The characters are flat and uninteresting, with little, well, _character_ to them. The story is nonsensical and contrived. There's no sense of continuity or space, we don't know what's going on, or where. Characters make stupid decisions that only work out because the screenwriter didn't think for a moment why it would be a bad idea. 

For example, when escaping from the Trade Federation ship, Qui-Gon and Obi-Won split up and smuggle aboard two different ships to the surface. Why do they do this? Wouldn't splitting up risk them being seperated. 

But none of that matters, because when they land, they immediately reunite. 

Now, there could be good tactical reasons why one _might_ choose to split up. If one is killed/captured, the other one has a chance of completing the mission. But these risks aren't conveyed to the audience. 

So if the line doesn't establish motivation, if it doesn't reveal character, if it doesn't advance the plot, if it doesn't forshadow or underline themes... what does it do? 

The answer is: Nothing. Every line needs to justify the audience's attention. 

There are some good things about Star Wars. The podrace is pretty good. It's a reasonably effective self-contained dramatic sequence. Clear objective, escalating obstacles, comprehensible physical geography.

It's just that the whole _getting_ to the podrace made no sense. _Very little_ in the movie made _any_ sense. 

Most of all, it's _boring._ A movie with lazer swords and blaster guns in _space_ is _boring._ I'm not being facetious by pointing that out: excitement and tension in a movie doesn't require millions of dollars of special effects, it doesn't require high concept, flashy action-adventure tropes. It requires _compression, clear stakes, a compelling protagonist, and dramatic escalation._ 

This is the difference between _spectacle_ and _drama_. 

Take, for example, the "duel of the fates" scene where Qui-Gon Jinn and Obi-Won fight Darth Maul. It's beautifully choreographed, beautifully shot, the VFX are amazing. But we know almost nothing about Darth Maul beyond his being an assassin working for the villains. He has no established personal relationship with Qui-Gon or Obi-Wan.

Compare that to the lightsaber duel between Obi-Won and Darth Vader in _Star Wars_, and even though it's a simple classic stage swordfight of the type they were doing in Shakespeare's day, there are stakes to the fight. There's _history_, there's _motivation_. The audience understands what the characters stand to lose.

In short, the comparison between those two lightsaber fights illustrates _precisely_ why spectacle _cannot_ substitute for drama. 

When you do have spectacle _and_ drama, together, they become _more powerful than you can possibly imagine._

In Back to the Future and My Cousin Vinny, practically every scene either advances the story, complicates the protagonist's situation, or raises the stakes. The Phantom Menace demonstrates what happens when a movie has plenty of incident but comparatively little dramatic progression.

Again, the Red Letter Media team did so much to explain what makes good film craft by pointing out exactly what happens when you _don't_ have good film craft. Through a lifetime of watching films, we have internalized the rhythms of good storytelling but often fail to notice when we go astray. By studying bad films, we start to recognize similar flaws in our own work.
