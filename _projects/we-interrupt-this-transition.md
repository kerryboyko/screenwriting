### Blurb

A reality show so exploitative, it was never supposed to happen.

British showrunner Sam Culver and semi-retired game show host Jimmy Howard pitch their latest project to the Garden Alpha streaming service. To make their real proposal look good, they throw in a deliberately outrageous idea: Woman Up!, a reality show where cisgender men compete to transition for a million-dollar prize.

They never expected executive Daria Bryant to say yes.

Now, Sam and Jimmy are stuck producing the most controversial reality show of the decade—dodging scandals, wrangling contestants, and desperately trying to keep their careers intact.

We Interrupt This Transition is a sharp-witted queer comedy that skewers the absurdity of reality TV, media exploitation, and gender politics.

### Writer's Note

*We Interrupt This Transition* was the first novel I ever wrote. And it has many of the flaws one would associate with a first effort.

For one, nobody told me how long a novel was supposed to be.

I published it serially on Scribblehub, and it ballooned to 230,000 words. If I ever publish a new edition, it's going to be a trilogy.

But for all its flaws, this was the first book where I really found my voice. And to explain why, I have to explain something about myself.

For most of my life, I honestly didn't believe I could write fiction.

I had trouble creating relatable characters. In fact, I had trouble *relating* to most of the supposedly relatable characters I encountered in fiction. I could understand their motivations intellectually, but there was always something missing. Something that made their experiences feel fundamentally different from my own.

Then, at the age of 43, I came out to myself as transgender.

And for the first time in my life, I started relating to *myself*.

Naturally, I gravitated towards trans narratives. But those were stories I had to go *looking for*.

For decades, the few trans characters I encountered in mainstream media were overwhelmingly victims, monsters, or jokes. I didn't see myself as any of those things. More importantly, I didn't want to be seen as *only* those things.

So I found myself exploring webcomics, fanfiction, and serialized trans literature. Stories that weren't necessarily available on bookshop shelves, but flourished in places like Archive of Our Own and Scribblehub.

And finally, I could see myself in fiction.

I found characters whose struggles resembled my own. Characters whose inner thoughts sounded like the ones I'd spent decades struggling to articulate. Characters written by people who seemed to understand something about me that I hadn't even understood about myself.

It unlocked something.

Suddenly, I could write fiction. And I've been writing a storm ever since.

There was just one problem.

I still had trouble finding a character I could *truly* relate to.

A great deal of the trans fiction I encountered focused on coming of age. High school, university, first relationships, first experiences of independence. And understandably so. These are enormously important stories, particularly for a community that has spent so long being denied the opportunity to tell them.

But they weren't *my* story.

I hadn't come out in high school. I hadn't figured myself out at university. I'd already spent decades building a life, establishing a career, and developing an identity that I presented to the world.

And I hadn't spent those decades knowingly hiding some great secret.

I *simply didn't know*.

I didn't have the language. I didn't have the stories. I didn't have the examples that might have helped me recognize myself.

So I wanted to write about a late bloomer.

Someone who had already built an entire life around an identity that didn't quite fit. Someone with a career, a reputation, even a public persona. Someone who was very, very good at making other people feel welcome, happy, and entertained, but who couldn't quite manage to feel those things for himself.

Thus, Jimmy Howard, Game Show Host, was born.

And because I was writing about a game show host, naturally I decided to surround him with the most ridiculous television industry satire I could come up with.

The premise resonated with Scribblehub readers almost immediately. So did the satire and humour. But what surprised me most was how much the community became part of the writing process.

People commented, speculated, argued, laughed, and occasionally noticed things about my characters before the characters themselves did.

Some of those conversations helped shape the story as I was writing it.

That's one of the reasons the original serialized edition, complete with its flaws, digressions, and embarrassing number of typos, remains available on Scribblehub. It isn't just an early draft. It's a record of the community that helped bring the book into existence.

And it's also why I think self-publishing was the right decision.

Trans fiction has historically been underserved by mainstream publishing, particularly stories that don't fit the familiar narratives of suffering, tragedy, or coming of age. Online communities offer something different: a place where readers can find stories that speak directly to their experiences, and where writers can experiment without first having to convince a publisher that those experiences are commercially viable.

I didn't write *We Interrupt This Transition* because I thought I'd found a gap in the market.

I wrote it because I'd found a gap on the bookshelf where I thought my own story ought to be.

It's too long. It's occasionally messy. There are things I'd write differently today. Indeed, I hope to have the chance to adapt it for television, so that I could take what I now know about the craft of screenwriting and apply it to the story. 

But it gave me Jimmy Howard. It gave me a community of readers. And, perhaps most importantly, it gave me the confidence to believe I could tell stories worth reading.

For a first novel, I think that's a pretty good result.
