### Synopsis

When young Angie Neer discovers that her local park is hosting a kite festival, she decides to build a kite of her own. Armed with a notebook, a few household materials, and boundless curiosity, she sets to work. Unfortunately, her first attempt barely gets off the ground.

Enter Artie Stick, her next-door neighbor and budding artist. Where Angie sees a problem to investigate, Artie sees something to create. Together, they begin experimenting with different materials, testing their designs, and learning from every failure. With encouragement from their families, the two young inventors combine scientific curiosity and artistic imagination to build a kite they can be proud of.

But when festival day arrives, the wind refuses to cooperate, and an approaching storm brings their dreams of flight crashing down.

Disappointed but undeterred, Angie soon discovers that everything they've learned might have another use. With a little ingenuity, some recycled materials, and a brand-new idea, the two friends turn their failed kite into a successful sailboat.

*Angie & Artie* is a gentle, dialogue-free children's story about friendship, creativity, and the joy of discovery, reminding us that even when an experiment fails, the knowledge we gain can take us somewhere unexpected.

### Writer's Note

*Angie & Artie* was originally created as an entry for the Sesame Workshop Writers' Room fellowship.

I also wanted to challenge myself by telling a story entirely without dialogue. There were two reasons for this: first, to make the story accessible to young children regardless of their language or reading ability; and second, to create something that could be enjoyed by children around the world without translation. The pictures tell the story.

As for the story itself, I wanted to create something educational and heartwarming. But rather than teach the same lessons I learned growing up, I wanted to share something I desperately wish someone had taught me as a child.

It is possible to try your hardest, to keep going in the face of failure... and still fail. 

And it's okay to be disappointed when that happens. But failure doesn't make you less of a person, nor does it mean your efforts were wasted. Even when things don't work out, we can learn something valuable from the experience.

The names "Angie Neer" and "Artie Stick" are puns, of course. But their friendship also reflects something I think our education system sometimes overlooks: the arts are every bit as important as STEM.

Science helps us understand how the world works. Art helps us imagine what else it could be. And when we bring the two together, we can discover things neither could accomplish alone.

One last thing: children's educational programming isn't just watched by children. Parents watch it too. And I wanted to show what it looks like when adults respect children's autonomy, including their right to make mistakes and experience disappointment.

I wrote the Neer and Stick parents to be supportive, but never to rescue Angie and Artie from failure. They help. They provide materials, demonstrate concepts, supervise experiments, and encourage the children. 

But the project remains Angie and Artie's. They don't build the kite for them. And when the festival goes badly, they don't manufacture a happy ending -- they allow Angie and Artie to be disappointed. 

Children deserve stories that respect their intelligence, their autonomy, and their emotional lives.
