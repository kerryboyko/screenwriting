_Pulp Fiction_. I don't know what the best movie ever made is, but ask most people to name their top ten and Pulp Fiction almost always makes the list. 

But why, though? Well, there's no doubt that it's _entertaining_. But a movie doesn't have to be _entertaining_ to be good. 

Here's the crazy thing. 1994 was a bumper crop for film. _Forrest Gump._ _The Shawshank Redemption_. _The Lion King_. 

And yet, _Pulp Fiction_ was (at least by my anecdotal reckoning) by far the most referenced in my M.A. Screenwriting programme, both by students and lecturers. 

Which is strange, when you consider that _Pulp Fiction_ violates a number of "rules" of feature filmmaking. The story isn't told in chronological order, most obviously. And much of the movie is spent in reflective dialogue _irrelevant_ to the plot. Think about how often Jules and Vincent go on tangents about hash bars, _Le Royale With Cheese_, the convoluted story of the origin of the gold watch... 

I'm firmly convinced that if Tarantino were to submit _Pulp Fiction_ to a script reader, they would almost certainly be told: "Tell the story chronologically, cut out the banter with Jules and Vincent in the hallway, and start with them knocking on the door and walking into Brett's apartment, and for God's sake cut the entire scene with Christopher Walken." 

But what Tarantino did... worked. 

Now, there are _many_ reasons why the movie worked, but I want to focus on two things. 

First, we spend a lot of time _understanding these characters as people._ 

What Koons's long story does is really explain why Butch is _willing to risk his life_ to retrieve his watch. The entire scene is _comically extravagant exposition._ But without it, Butch's decision to return for his watch becomes nothing more than an "idiot plot" ­— a character doing something stupid because the writer needs him somewhere dangerous. 

What the long lead-up to Brett's apartment does is explain that the hitmen we are following have their own lives, ambitions. We learn about their character, we get an idea of their wants and needs, and we start to get hints at Vincent's tragic flaws, and how Jules develops his arc. Most crime stories focus on the _plot_, whereas Tarantino chose to focus on the _characters._ By spending so much _human_ time with our criminals, our empathy goes towards them even while they do objectively bad things. This increases our "pity and fear" for them. 

In many ways, this is an excellent example of how dialogue that doesn't advance the plot can still serve a dramatic purpose. The Royale with Cheese conversation doesn't advance the plot. But it establishes character, relationships, familiarity, tone, and normality. That makes the transition into violence extremely effective. We have established the key rule of this world: Murder is mundane to our characters. 

Tarantino manages to communicate that without anyone having to come out and actually _say_ it.

Screenwriters are frequently encouraged to do the narrative equivalent of filling uncomfortable silences with conversation. Every moment _must_ advance the plot. Every scene _must_ push the story forward. Every exchange _must_ accomplish something immediately identifiable. 

Yet, Tarantino is comfortable allowing his characters simply to exist, so long as their existence reveals character, theme, or tone. 

But what's really, _really_ interesting is how, by telling the story out of order, the audience knows more about the characters' fates than the characters do. It establishes "dramatic irony": where the audience of the work knows something the characters do not. _The rearrangement of the narrative fundamentally changes the meaning of the narrative._

Dramatic irony is a great form of tension. Think about _Die Hard_, when John McClane and Hans Gruber meet for the first time. We know who Gruber is, McClane does not, and we're just waiting for the shoe to drop — explicit dramatic irony. 

(It could also be argued that audience _expectation_ established by genre can also lead to a "meta" form of dramatic irony... the audience expects Hans Gruber to lose, which Gruber obviously does not. It is a prediction informed by genre conventions. Both influence our experience of tension, but they operate somewhat differently.)

Another classic example of dramatic irony: Hitchcock's aphorism about the "bomb under the table." 

Two people are having breakfast. Then a bomb explodes. That's "surprise." 

Two people are having breakfast. The audience sees that there's a bomb under the table. That's _suspense_. It's also dramatic irony. 

The biggest dramatic irony point of the movie is when Vincent is killed by Butch in segment 5, "The Gold Watch". Then we move to segment six, "The Bonnie Situation" where we see the immediate aftermath of their retrieval of Marsellus Wallace's briefcase: Here, Vincent is shown alive alongside Jules. 

Without the knowledge of Vincent's now inevitable death, the entire context for the conversation about Jules choosing to retire because he felt he had been the recipient of a miracle, and Vincent's determination to continue changes. We know Vincent is headed on a path to ruin. Jules is trying to step away from that path. That changes everything that comes afterwards. 

We see how Vincent's poor decision-making and recklessness lead to the death of Marvin, and how that has led to danger for both Vincent and Jules. It is Vincent, not Jules, who questions Winston Wolf's methods. Everything acquires more poignancy because we _know_ where Vincent's reckless behavior will lead: to him being so blasé about death and violence that he doesn't even think to take the gun into the bathroom with him on the Butch hit, which allows Butch to kill him. 

We lose that, if the story is told in sequence. 

What's interesting about Pulp Fiction is that for all its unconventional storytelling, it actually _somehow_ tells a full three-act structure story. The first act establishes the status quo: This is a world of cinematic violence, our characters are hitmen who have become desensitized to the life and death consequences of their jobs, Marsellus Wallace is _not to be f\*\*ked with_ and Vincent has been tasked with showing his wife, Mia, a good time. 

And at the end, with Jules and Vincent facing off against Honey Bunny and Pumpkin in the diner, we see the third act resolution — Jules has truly changed, letting the two amateur thieves walk away, so long as they leave the briefcase. Vincent hasn't, which will lead to his downfall. 

But what about that second act? 

What's interesting about Pulp Fiction is that it has _three second acts_. The first second act is the Mia Wallace arc. The second second act is the Gold Watch arc. And the third second act is the Bonnie Situation arc.  

As a thought experiment, with the exception that we learn of Vincent's ultimate fate in the Gold Watch arc, would this movie have worked if we were shown only _one_ of those arcs? 

I'd argue yes. It wouldn't be as good, certainly. It wouldn't be considered a work of genius. Probably would have been beaten out by _Three Colours: Red_ for the Palme d'Or at Cannes that year. But it would have worked. 

And what's strange is that I think it would have worked _no matter which second act_ you selected. And the story is the same: each involves a character confronting a situation that cannot be resolved through their usual relationship with violence... in fact if there is a running theme throughout the second act, it is this: _violence is not the right answer_. 

* Act I: Shared Establishment - The criminal world of Marsellus Wallace
  * Act II-A — Mia Wallace / Temptation and consequences — Vincent encounters a crisis in which his capacity for violence is essentially useless.
  * Act II-B — The Gold Watch / Loyalty and redemption — Butch ultimately chooses to save someone he has every reason to despise and fear.
  * Act II-C — The Bonnie Situation / Recklessness and transformation — Jules begins questioning whether violence should be his profession at all.
* Act III: Shared thematic resolution — Jules chooses to become a different person and leave violence behind. 

The diner sequence in Act III only contains Jules, but it provides the _thematic_ resolution to all three plotlines: a man perfectly capable of killing someone consciously chooses not to. The film spends most of its running time establishing violence as mundane, only to make an act of nonviolence its dramatic climax.

And that's the genius of _Pulp Fiction_. The same setup and resolution can be used to tell three different, interconnected, stories, and telling all of them allows us insight into the others that we wouldn't otherwise have. 

A naive interpretation of this would be that "genius filmmakers can break the rules." That's... true, but not the whole story. What I think the greater takeaway from _Pulp Fiction_ is that some apparent violations of screenwriting rules are actually sophisticated applications of more fundamental dramatic principles. Tarantino sacrifices chronological clarity to strengthen dramatic irony, sacrifices narrative economy to strengthen characterization, and sacrifices conventional unity of action to strengthen _thematic_ unity. 

In other words: yeah, it really is a m\*\*\*\*\*f\*\*\*ing good movie.
