I firmly believe this is a must-watch for any film student. And then after they've watched it, they should immediately consult the [Red Letter Media review of the movie](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FxKtZmQgxrI). 

That review is a film school education in and of itself. 

One particular excercise they did was ask people to describe the characters of _Phantom Menace_ and the original _Star Wars_ without describing their outfit or job in the movie. 

Han Solo: cocky thief with a heart of gold. C3PO: Prissy worrier, bumbling comic relief. 

Then they tried to do it with Qui-Gon Jinn or Princess Amidala. Crickets. 

In truth, we in filmmaking are _spoiled_ by our choice of medium. It is _expensive_ to make a film. So it is extremely rare to see a film _this bad_ and _this accessible_. Hollywood's financial gatekeeping produces a kind of survivorship bias. Most films that get substantial budgets have already passed through multiple layers of creative scrutiny. Even mediocre films tend to exhibit some baseline professional competence.

_The Phantom Menace_ is a particularly interesting case study. Unlike _Plan 9 from Outer Space_, its director wasn't constrained by budget. Unlike _The Room_, it wasn't made by an inexperienced amateur. George Lucas had already revolutionized cinema, had virtually unlimited resources, some of the finest technical artists in the world, and extraordinary creative control. 

And yet, it gets _almost none_ of the screenwriting basics right. The characters are flat and uninteresting, with little, well, _character_ to them. The story is nonsensical and contrived. There's no sense of continuity or space, we don't know what's going on, or where. Characters make stupid decisions that only work out because the screenwriter didn't think for a moment why it would be a bad idea. 

For example, when escaping from the Trade Federation ship, Qui-Gon and Obi-Won split up and smuggle aboard two different ships to the surface. Why do they do this? Wouldn't splitting up risk them being seperated. 

But none of that matters, because when they land, they immediately reunite. 

Now, there could be good tactical reasons why one _might_ choose to split up. If one is killed/captured, the other one has a chance of completing the mission. But these risks aren't conveyed to the audience. 

So if the line doesn't establish motivation, if it doesn't reveal character, if it doesn't advance the plot, if it doesn't forshadow or underline themes... what does it do? 

The answer is: Nothing. Every line needs to justify the audience's attention. 

There are some good things about Star Wars. The podrace is pretty good. It's a reasonably effective self-contained dramatic sequence. Clear objective, escalating obstacles, comprehensible physical geography.

It's just that the whole _getting_ to the podrace made no sense. _Very little_ in the movie made _any_ sense. 

Most of all, it's _boring._ A movie with lazer swords and blaster guns in _space_ is _boring._ I'm not being facetious by pointing that out: excitement and tension in a movie doesn't require millions of dollars of special effects, it doesn't require high concept, flashy action-adventure tropes. It requires _compression, clear stakes, a compelling protagonist, and dramatic escalation._ 

This is the difference between _spectacle_ and _drama_. 

Take, for example, the "duel of the fates" scene where Qui-Gon Jinn and Obi-Won fight Darth Maul. It's beautifully choreographed, beautifully shot, the VFX are amazing. But we know almost nothing about Darth Maul beyond his being an assassin working for the villains. He has no established personal relationship with Qui-Gon or Obi-Wan.

Compare that to the lightsaber duel between Obi-Won and Darth Vader in _Star Wars_, and even though it's a simple classic stage swordfight of the type they were doing in Shakespeare's day, there are stakes to the fight. There's _history_, there's _motivation_. The audience understands what the characters stand to lose.

In short, the comparison between those two lightsaber fights illustrates _precisely_ why spectacle _cannot_ substitute for drama. 

When you do have spectacle _and_ drama, together, they become _more powerful than you can possibly imagine._

In Back to the Future and My Cousin Vinny, practically every scene either advances the story, complicates the protagonist's situation, or raises the stakes. The Phantom Menace demonstrates what happens when a movie has plenty of incident but comparatively little dramatic progression.

Again, the Red Letter Media team did so much to explain what makes good film craft by pointing out exactly what happens when you _don't_ have good film craft. Through a lifetime of watching films, we have internalized the rhythms of good storytelling but often fail to notice when we go astray. By studying bad films, we start to recognize similar flaws in our own work.
