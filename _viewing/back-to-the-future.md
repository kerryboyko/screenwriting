_Back To The Future_ is not the best movie ever made. It's not my favorite. But from a screenwriting craft perspective, it's hard to find a better example. 

Every line of dialogue, every bit of action is _doing something._ It's a movie about time and it doesn't waste any of it. Things often do two or three things at once. For example, early on, Mr. Strickland doesn't just admonish Marty McFly for being late to school. He says:

Look at what we're learning from that one exchange: 

* Authority figures see Marty McFly as someone who will never succeed.
* Marty wants to prove them wrong. 
* His father is someone who didn't succeed. 
* Marty McFly plays in a band (setting up his use of his guitar playing skills to get his parents to fall in love later in the film.)
* "History is gonna change" is the entire premise of the movie -- and it sets up both literal and dramatic irony. We know this is a time travel movie. Marty doesn't. So when he says that, he doesn't realize how on-the-nose he's being. 

Pick any scene in _Back To The Future_ and it will often be doing multiple things at once. When Marty wakes his father from a dead sleep using Van Halen, it not only advances the plot, it also establishes Marty's character as an outside-the-box thinker _and_ provides a comedic beat _and_ provides character motivation for George _and_ sets up the joke at the end of the movie where it serves as inspiration for George's sci-fi novel. 

And it's got dead-on three-act structure and pacing. Act I sets up Marty as down on his luck and establishes the circumstances. We break into Act II when he travels accidentally to 1955. In the middle, (literally... this scene ends at the 56:00 mark of a 1:12:00 film... not including the credits), we get the big mid-point turn:

* Marty shows Doc the video he took in 1985. 
* Doc learns the DeLorean requires 1.21 gigawatts, and establishes that finding a power source for that in 1955 is impossible, except for a bolt of lightning... "except you don't know where and when one will strike." 
* Then Marty mentions that he has a life in 1985, and a girlfriend. "Look what she wrote," he says, showing Doc the very same piece of paper that has the "Save The Clocktower" fundraiser on it, which lists *exactly* when a lightning bolt will strike. 
  * Notably, the audience gets to see the "Save The Clocktower" text clearly in frame. 
* Doc then warns Marty not to interact with anything in 1955, lest he change the future. 
* That's when Marty mentions running into his parents, and Doc establishes through the "erasing" picture that Marty has accidentally erased himself from the timeline. 

The film has already established: 

* that the DeLorean needs an extraordinary amount of electricity to travel through time, 
* that lightning struck Hill Valley's clock tower at a particular time in 1955. 

Until this scene, however, those two facts haven't been connected. And the connection comes from something seemingly unrelated to either one: Marty's girlfriend.

Marty shows Doc the message Jennifer wrote on the clock tower flyer, reminding us of what he stands to lose if he can't return to 1985. But the very thing that establishes the emotional stakes also provides the solution to the practical problem. The flyer tells Doc exactly when and where lightning will strike.

And just as they've discovered a way home, the screenplay introduces another complication: Marty has inadvertently endangered his own existence.

There is no fat in the movie. Everything does multiple things at once. 

It took a _lot_ of hard work to write a script _that tightly packed_ with _everything_ you need, when you need it. 

I think if you watch _one_ film, just _one,_ this is the film that most clearly shows off the _craft skills_ you are going to film school to learn.
